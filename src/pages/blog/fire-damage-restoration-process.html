Short answer: The fire damage restoration process runs in this order: inspection and assessment, board-up and roof tarping, water removal and drying, debris and tear-out, soot and smoke cleaning, contents cleaning, odor removal, and finally repairs and rebuilding. Cleanup after a small fire may take one to two weeks; a fire that burned structure can take several months including repairs.

Knowing what's coming makes a hard time a little easier. Crews arrive, windows get boarded, fans and air scrubbers run, walls may be opened up, and then the rebuild starts. Every job is different, but the order is nearly always the same. This guide explains each stage of fire damage restoration in plain words, so you can follow along and ask the right questions.

What is fire damage restoration?

Fire damage restoration is everything needed to return a home or business to its condition before the fire. It has two halves. Mitigation stops the damage from getting worse. Restoration and repair clean what can be saved and rebuild what can't. The industry standard for the cleaning side is ANSI/IICRC S700, first published in 2025. It covers inspection, cleaning and odor control, but not reconstruction, which is handled under building codes.

The fire damage restoration process, step by step

  1. Emergency contact and release. Work starts after the fire department releases the property. You call the restoration company and your insurer.
  2. Inspection and assessment. The crew walks every room, attic and crawl space. They map fire, smoke, soot and water damage, check the type of soot, and test older materials for lead and asbestos where needed.
  3. Board-up and roof tarping. Broken windows, doors and roof holes are covered so weather and intruders stay out.
  4. Water removal and drying. Water from fire hoses is pumped out, and air movers and dehumidifiers dry the structure.
  5. Debris removal and tear-out. Burned materials, wet insulation and drywall that can't be saved are removed.
  6. Soot and smoke cleaning. Every surface smoke touched is cleaned with HEPA vacuums, dry chemical sponges and the right wet cleaners for the soot type.
  7. Contents cleaning. Belongings are packed out, cleaned and stored, or cleaned on site after small fires.
  8. Odor removal. HVAC ducts are cleaned, then thermal fogging, hydroxyl or ozone treatment neutralizes odor that's left.
  9. Repairs and rebuilding. Framing, roofing, drywall, wiring, floors and paint are rebuilt with permits and inspections.

Why drying and cleaning happen fast

Two things get worse with time: water and soot. Fire hoses can leave a lot of water in a house. The EPA says wet building materials should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. Soot is acidic, so it keeps etching metal, glass and stone the longer it sits. That's why mitigation happens in the first days, even before every repair decision is made.

How long does fire damage restoration take?

How long fire damage restoration takes depends mostly on whether the structure burned.

  • Small kitchen fire, smoke in a few rooms: cleaning and odor work often take one to two weeks, plus minor repairs.
  • Fire in one room with smoke through the house: several weeks, including tear-out and rebuilding that room.
  • Burned framing, roof or wiring: often three to nine months, including permits, insurance approval and material lead times.
  • Total loss: a full rebuild, often a year or more.

Waiting on insurance approval and permits is often the longest part. Good documentation and a clear scope of work speed both up.

Safety steps inside the process

Older homes add steps. The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule requires paid contractors who disturb painted surfaces in homes built before 1978 to follow lead-safe work practices. Older ceiling texture, flooring and pipe wrap may contain asbestos and should be tested before tear-out.

Odor treatment has its own rules. The EPA warns that ozone can damage the lungs and that no federal agency has approved ozone generators for occupied spaces. When crews use ozone after a fire, the home must be empty and aired out afterward. Our guide to hydroxyl generators, ozone and thermal fogging explains the options.

Your role during restoration

  • Approve the written scope before work starts.
  • Keep your adjuster in the loop on any added damage.
  • Point out heirlooms and important papers before pack-out.
  • Keep receipts for living expenses.
  • Do a final walk-through and check every room against the scope.

When should you call a fire damage restoration professional?

  • Firefighters put water into the house.
  • Smoke or soot reached more than one room.
  • The roof, windows or doors are open.
  • Structure, wiring or the HVAC system may be damaged.
