Short answer: What is soot? It is the fine black or brown powder left when something burns without enough oxygen. It is mostly carbon, mixed with tars, oils, acids and metals from whatever burned. Yes, soot can be dangerous: its particles are small enough to reach deep into the lungs, and the National Cancer Institute says it may contain carcinogens such as arsenic, cadmium and chromium.

If you have seen black film on walls after a fire, a dark ring above a candle, or streaks around a furnace vent, you have seen soot. It looks like simple dirt, but it behaves differently. It smears, stains and corrodes, and it carries chemicals you don't want to breathe or touch. This guide explains what soot is, what's in it, and how to stay safe. When soot covers more than a small spot, professional soot removal is the safer way to get it out.

What is soot?

Soot is a byproduct of incomplete burning. The National Cancer Institute describes it as the result of incomplete burning of carbon-containing materials such as wood, fuel oil, plastics and household trash. When a fire doesn't get enough oxygen, carbon doesn't fully turn into gas. Instead, tiny carbon particles form, cling together and travel with the smoke until they settle on surfaces.

That's why a house fire leaves soot far from the flames. Hot smoke carries particles up stairwells, through ducts and into closets, where they cool and stick.

What is in soot?

Soot is mostly carbon, but its exact makeup depends on what burned. A house fire burns wood, drywall paper, carpet, foam, plastics, wiring and food all at once, so its soot is a complex mix.

Is soot dangerous?

Yes, soot is a real health risk, mainly because of particle size and the chemicals it carries. The EPA says fine particles 2.5 micrometers and smaller pose the greatest risk to health. For scale, the EPA notes an average human hair is about 70 micrometers wide, 30 times larger than the largest fine particle. Particles this small can get deep into the lungs, and some can enter the bloodstream.

Soot is also linked to cancer with long exposure. The National Toxicology Program lists soots as known human carcinogens. The National Cancer Institute notes that chimney sweeps, who have the highest work exposure, have an increased risk of skin and other cancers. A one-time house fire is a very different exposure from a career of chimney work, but it's a good reason to avoid breathing or touching soot.

People can be exposed by breathing soot, swallowing it or absorbing it through skin, according to the National Cancer Institute. Children, older adults and people with heart or lung disease should stay away from soot cleanup.

Types of soot in a home

Different fires leave different soot. The type decides how it must be cleaned.

  • Dry soot: powdery, from hot, fast fires that burn wood and paper. Easiest to clean with HEPA vacuums and dry chemical sponges.
  • Wet (oily) soot: sticky, from smoldering plastics, rubber and foam. Smears badly and needs degreasers.
  • Protein residue: a thin yellow film from burned food, with a strong, lasting smell.
  • Fuel-oil soot: black and oily, from an oil furnace puff-back that can coat a whole house.

Where soot comes from at home (without a big fire)

Soot isn't only a fire problem. Smaller sources build it up over time.

The NFPA ranks heating equipment just behind cooking as a cause of home fires. Black soot around any furnace, boiler or gas appliance is a sign to shut it off and have it checked.

Why soot damages surfaces

Soot is acidic, so it keeps working after the fire is out. Left in place, it pits chrome and brass, etches glass, yellows white plastics and stains grout and stone. The longer it sits, the more items move from "clean" to "replace." Getting soot off within days protects belongings and lowers repair costs. How to handle soot safely

  1. Wait for clearance. Don't enter a fire-damaged home until the fire department says it's safe.
  2. Gear up. Wear gloves, long sleeves and a well-fitted respirator. The EPA says surgical masks and one-strap dust masks won't protect your lungs; use one marked N95 or P100.
  3. Keep air still. Don't use fans or the HVAC system while cleaning; they spread soot. The EPA also advises never using a leaf blower on ash.
  4. Vacuum first. Use a HEPA vacuum. Regular vacuums blow fine particles back into the air.
  5. Don't wipe wet. Water first smears soot deeper into paint and fabric.
  6. Wash up. Wash skin and clothes after cleanup.

How to prevent soot buildup

  • Trim candle wicks to about a quarter inch and keep candles out of drafts.
  • Have chimneys swept and inspected every year if you burn wood.
  • Service oil burners and gas appliances annually.
  • Burn only dry, seasoned firewood.
  • Replace HVAC filters on schedule.

Local note: Oily puff-back soot is common where homes burn fuel oil, such as Maine, New Hampshire and Alaska. Wood stove and chimney soot shows up most in Vermont and Montana, where wood heat is common.

  • Soot got into the HVAC system or electronics.
  • Anyone in the home has asthma or lung disease.
