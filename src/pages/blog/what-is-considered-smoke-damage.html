Short answer: Smoke damage is any staining, residue, odor, discoloration or corrosion that smoke leaves on a building or belongings. It is considered smoke damage even if flames never touched the room. Common signs are black or yellow film on walls, a lingering smell, dark streaks near vents, and tarnished metal. Most home and renters policies cover sudden smoke damage.

After a fire, it's easy to see what burned. Smoke damage is harder to spot, and it often covers far more of the house. It can show up in closets, upstairs bedrooms and inside cabinets long after the fire is out. Knowing what counts helps you document a claim and decide when to call for smoke damage restoration. This is general information, not insurance advice; every policy differs.

What counts as smoke damage?

Anything smoke touched and changed counts. That includes the building and everything in it. These are the most common types:

  • Surface film: gray, black or yellowish residue on walls, ceilings, windows and furniture.
  • Staining: discoloration on paint, grout, stone, plastics and fabrics that won't wipe off.
  • Odor: smoke smell in rooms, fabrics, insulation and ducts.
  • Corrosion: pitting or tarnish on metal fixtures, hinges and appliances, caused by acidic soot.
  • HVAC contamination: soot in ducts, filters and the air handler.
  • Electronics damage: soot inside computers, TVs and appliances that can cause failures later.

Smoke damage vs. fire damage

Fire damage is what flames and heat destroyed: charred framing, burned drywall, melted fixtures. Smoke damage is the residue and odor smoke leaves, which can reach every floor. A small kitchen fire may cause only a little fire damage but heavy smoke damage through the whole house. Insurers usually handle both under the same claim.

Is smoke damage harmful to your health?

Smoke damage can be. Smoke and soot contain fine particles. The EPA says particles 2.5 micrometers and smaller, called PM2.5, pose the greatest risk to health, and some can reach deep into the lungs and even the bloodstream. Ash from burned buildings may contain metals and other harmful chemicals, according to the EPA. People with asthma, heart or lung disease, older adults and children should stay away from cleanup.

Wildfire smoke damage

Wildfire smoke can damage a home miles from the flames. It leaves ash on floors and sills, a film on walls and windows, and odor in fabrics and HVAC systems. Many policies treat wildfire smoke the same as smoke from a house fire, as long as the damage is real and documented. Keep dirty HVAC filters and photograph ash before you clean.

What is considered smoke damage for insurance?

For insurance, smoke damage usually means sudden, accidental damage from a fire or similar event. The NAIC explains that homeowners policies cover the dwelling, other structures, personal property and loss of use. Smoke damage can fall under any of these.

United Policyholders notes that smoke is typically a covered peril and that insurers usually pay to clean smoke and ash. Disputes often come down to clean or replace. Porous items such as fabrics, rugs, curtains and unfinished wood may need replacing. If a reputable cleaner says an item can't be cleaned, or it still smells after cleaning, it may be replaced.

How to document smoke damage

  1. Photograph every room, including inside closets, cabinets and drawers.
  2. Swipe a white cloth on walls and surfaces and photograph the soot on it.
  3. Keep the dirty HVAC filter in a bag as evidence.
  4. Write down odor by room, since smell doesn't show in photos.
  5. List damaged items before you clean or throw anything away.

United Policyholders also suggests telling your adjuster right away if anyone in the home has asthma or allergies, and not accepting a shortcut such as being told to simply wipe surfaces yourself.

How to limit smoke damage after a fire

  • Keep the HVAC off until filters and ducts are checked.
  • Don't wipe soot with a wet cloth; it smears.
  • Get soot off metal, stone and electronics within days.
  • Bag smoky fabrics and move them out of the house.

When should you call a smoke damage professional?

  • Soot film or stains cover more than one room.
  • Smoke smell returns when the HVAC runs.
  • Metal fixtures or electronics have soot on them.
  • The residue smears or is oily.
  • You need a documented scope for your insurer.
