Short answer: What to do after a house fire: first make sure everyone is safe and don't go back in until the fire department says it's safe. Then call your insurance company, find a place to stay, get the fire report, secure the property with board-up, and document everything before you clean or throw anything away. Keep every receipt.

The hours after a fire are a blur. You may be standing outside in borrowed clothes, with no idea where to sleep or who to call. That's normal. You don't have to do everything at once, but a few early steps protect your family, your home and your insurance claim. This checklist puts them in order. What to do in the first 24 hours after a house fire

  1. Make sure everyone is safe. Account for every person and pet. Get medical care for anyone who breathed smoke or was burned.
  2. Stay out until cleared. Don't go back in until the fire department says it's safe. Fire can rekindle, and floors, ceilings and stairs may be weak.
  3. Get emergency help. The American Red Cross can help with emergency shelter, food, clothing and medicine. Call your local chapter or ask the fire department to connect you.
  4. Call your insurance company. Report the fire, get a claim number and ask what they need from you. Ask how additional living expenses work under your policy.
  5. Tell key people. Notify your landlord if you rent, your mortgage lender, and family. The U.S. Fire Administration also suggests telling the police if the home will be empty.
  6. Secure the property. Arrange board-up and roof tarping so weather and intruders stay out.
  7. Find a place to stay. A friend, a hotel or a short-term rental. Keep every receipt.

What to do after a house fire: the first week

  1. Get the fire report. Ask the fire department for a copy or the report number. Your insurer will want it.
  2. Walk through with care. Once cleared, the American Red Cross advises checking for structural damage, and not forcing a jammed door, which may be holding up part of the structure.
  3. Don't touch the utilities. Leave gas, power and water to the utility company or a licensed contractor. Never turn gas back on yourself.
  4. Document everything. Photograph and video every room, inside cabinets and closets. Do this before cleaning.
  5. Start your inventory. List damaged belongings by room. United Policyholders suggests keeping a claim diary of every conversation with your insurer.
  6. Throw out food. The Red Cross advises discarding food exposed to heat, smoke or soot. Photograph it first.
  7. Find valuables and documents. Look for IDs, cash, jewelry and papers. The U.S. Fire Administration's After the Fire guide explains how to replace burned documents and handle damaged money.
  8. Meet the adjuster. Walk the house together and share your photos and list.
  9. Get cleanup started. Water from firefighting, soot and smoke all get worse with time.

What not to do after a house fire

  • Don't enter before the fire department clears the house.
  • Don't throw away damaged items before they are photographed and listed.
  • Don't turn on damaged appliances, electronics or the HVAC system.
  • Don't wipe soot with a wet cloth; it smears.
  • Don't sign a contract with a company that showed up uninvited before you check it out.
  • Don't accept a fast settlement before you know the full damage.

Where to stay after a house fire

If the house isn't livable, check your policy for additional living expenses (ALE), sometimes called loss of use. The NAIC explains that many homeowners policies pay the extra cost of living elsewhere, such as a hotel, above your normal expenses while the home is repaired. Renters policies usually include ALE too.

Keep receipts for lodging, meals out, laundry and extra mileage. Ask your insurer early whether there's a daily or total limit.

Staying safe during cleanup

Soot and ash can hold harmful residue. The EPA advises wearing gloves, long sleeves, long pants and an N95 or P100 respirator around ash, and says surgical masks won't protect your lungs. Keep children away from ash and burned areas. Don't use a leaf blower or dry-sweep; mist ash first, then wet mop.

Water from fire hoses is its own problem. Wet drywall, insulation and floors can grow mold if they aren't dried quickly.

Dealing with insurance after a house fire

Your claim will move faster with good records. Get a full copy of your policy, not just the declarations page, and read the deadlines. Put important requests in writing. United Policyholders recommends a claim diary with names, dates and what was said. What happens next: cleanup and repairs

Once the house is secure, restoration usually follows the same order: water removal and drying, debris removal, soot and smoke cleaning, odor removal, contents cleaning and then repairs. The fire damage restoration process guide walks through each step and how long it takes.

How to be ready for the next emergency

  • Keep a home inventory with photos stored in the cloud.
  • Store copies of IDs and policies off site or online.
  • Test smoke alarms monthly.
  • Plan two ways out of every room and a meeting spot outside.

Local note: After a wildfire in California or Colorado, ash cleanup and smoke odor are the main tasks. In winter fires in Minnesota or North Dakota, protecting pipes from freezing after the heat goes out comes first.
