Quick answer: Fire board-up covers broken windows, doors and wall openings with plywood, and emergency roof tarping covers holes and burned roofing with heavy tarps. It is the first job after the fire department releases a property. It keeps out rain, animals and intruders, and the U.S. Fire Administration notes you may need to board up so no one can get in.

What board-up and roof tarping include

  • Window and door board-up with exterior-grade plywood, screwed or bolted, never just nailed to trim.
  • Secure entry door with a lock, so you and your adjuster can get in.
  • Emergency roof tarping over vent holes and burned sections, with tarps fastened to battens.
  • Wall openings sealed where siding or sheathing burned through.
  • Temporary bracing or shoring if a wall, porch or ceiling is weak.
  • Fencing or barricade tape around hazards, where local rules require.
  • Photos before and after for your insurance claim.

Why firefighters leave openings behind

Firefighters break windows and cut holes in roofs on purpose. Venting lets hot gases and smoke escape so they can get inside and put the fire out. Doors may be forced open. It is normal and it saves the structure, but once crews leave, those openings are your responsibility. Rain through a roof hole can cause more damage in one storm than the fire did to the rooms below.

The board-up and tarping process

  1. Wait for release. Crews start after the fire department releases the property. The American Red Cross advises not forcing a jammed door open, because it may be holding up part of the house.
  2. Safety check. The crew looks for loose power lines, gas smells, weak floors and roof sections before climbing or entering.
  3. Measure and cut. Plywood is cut to cover each opening with overlap onto solid framing.
  4. Fasten. Panels are screwed or bolted through from the inside where possible, so they cannot be pried off.
  5. Tarp the roof. Heavy tarps are run past the ridge, wrapped around wood battens and fastened, so wind cannot get underneath.
  6. Lock up and document. One door gets a lock, keys go to you, and photos go into the job file.

Why securing the property matters for insurance

Most policies expect you to protect the property from more damage after a loss. If rain gets in through an open roof days after a fire, the insurer may question the extra damage. Boarding up quickly, keeping the invoice and photos, and telling your insurer protects the claim. The U.S. Fire Administration also suggests notifying the police if the home will be empty.

Staying somewhere else while the house is secured

A boarded house is rarely livable. Ask your insurer about additional living expenses. The NAIC explains that many homeowners policies pay temporary housing costs, such as a hotel, above your normal living expenses while the home is repaired. The American Red Cross can help with a place to stay the first night.

What affects the cost of board-up and tarping

  • The number and size of openings.
  • Roof pitch, height and how much roofing burned.
  • Upper-floor work that needs ladders or lifts.
  • Night, weekend or storm-condition work.
  • Bracing or shoring if part of the structure is weak.

DIY or call a professional?

A single ground-floor window can be covered with plywood and screws once the house is released. Leave roof tarping, upper floors and any opening near burned framing or wiring to a crew. Falls from fire-damaged roofs are a real risk, since decking that looks solid may be charred underneath.

After board-up: what comes next

Once the property is secure, the next steps are fire damage cleanup to remove water and debris, then soot and smoke cleaning, and finally fire damage repair to replace windows, doors and roofing for good.
