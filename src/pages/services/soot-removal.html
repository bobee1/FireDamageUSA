Quick answer: Soot removal cleans the black residue fire leaves on walls, ceilings, cabinets and belongings. The right method depends on the soot: dry soot comes off with HEPA vacuums and dry chemical sponges, while oily, wet and protein residue needs degreasers. Testing first matters, because the wrong cleaner smears soot deeper into paint.

What soot removal includes

  • Soot identification and test spots on each surface type.
  • HEPA vacuuming of loose soot from walls, ceilings, floors and fabrics.
  • Dry chemical sponge cleaning of flat paint and textured ceilings.
  • Wet cleaning with degreasers and alkaline cleaners where testing allows.
  • Cabinet, appliance and fixture cleaning, inside and out.
  • Metal and glass protection to stop acidic soot from pitting and etching.
  • Sealing with a stain-blocking primer before repainting.

The soot removal process

  1. Protect and contain. Floors, unaffected rooms and HVAC returns are covered, and HEPA air scrubbers start.
  2. Test. The crew tests a small spot on each surface to choose the cleaning order and products.
  3. Vacuum loose soot. HEPA vacuums with soft brushes lift loose particles without rubbing.
  4. Dry clean. Dry chemical sponges are wiped in long, one-direction strokes on walls and ceilings.
  5. Wet clean. Degreasing or alkaline cleaners remove what is left, rinsed and dried.
  6. Detail and protect. Fixtures, hinges, glass and metal are cleaned and polished so acids stop working on them.
  7. Seal and paint. A sealing primer locks in stains and odor, then finish paint goes on.

Why soot should be removed quickly

Soot is acidic. Left in place it keeps reacting with what it touches: it pits chrome and brass, etches window glass, yellows plastics and white appliances, and stains grout and porous stone. The longer it sits, the more items move from "clean" to "replace". The U.S. Fire Administration notes that items that did not burn may still be ruined by smoke or water, and that anything you want to keep needs careful cleaning.

Furnace puff-back soot

A puff-back happens when an oil furnace or boiler misfires and blows oily soot through the ducts. It can coat every room in a fine black film in seconds. Heating equipment is a leading cause of home fires, according to the NFPA, and these fires peak in cold months. Puff-back cleanup needs the furnace serviced first, then whole-house soot removal, duct cleaning and contents cleaning. Fuel-oil soot smears badly with water, so testing is essential.

Common soot removal mistakes

  • Wiping with a wet rag first, which drives soot into paint.
  • Using a regular vacuum or shop vac, which blows fine particles back into the air.
  • Running the HVAC system before ducts and filters are cleaned.
  • Painting over soot without a sealing primer.
  • Scrubbing textured "popcorn" ceilings, which may also contain asbestos in older homes.

What affects the cost of soot removal

  • Square footage and ceiling height.
  • Soot type: oily and fuel-oil soot take far longer than dry soot.
  • Surface type: textured, porous and flat-paint surfaces are slower to clean.
  • How many cabinets, fixtures and belongings need cleaning.
  • Whether ducts and the HVAC system need cleaning too.

DIY or call a professional?

A little candle soot on one wall can be cleaned with a dry sponge and a mild detergent. The American Red Cross suggests rubber gloves and a mild soap or detergent for walls, furniture and floors. Call a professional when soot covers more than one room, when it smears, when it came from a puff-back, or when it got into the HVAC system.

Soot removal and insurance

Photograph soot before cleaning, including inside cabinets and closets, and on the HVAC filter. Soot cleanup after a sudden fire or puff-back is usually part of a homeowners claim, but check your policy and talk with your insurer before large jobs.
