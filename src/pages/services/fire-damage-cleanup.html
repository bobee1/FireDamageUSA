Quick answer: Fire damage cleanup removes what the fire and the firefighters left behind: burned debris, soaked carpet and drywall, standing water and loose ash. It comes before soot cleaning and repairs. The EPA says wet materials dried within 24 to 48 hours usually will not grow mold, so cleanup should start as soon as the building is released.

What fire damage cleanup includes

  • Site safety check of floors, ceilings, stairs and utilities before work starts.
  • Board-up and tarping of broken windows, doors and roof openings.
  • Water extraction with pumps and extractors, then drying with air movers and dehumidifiers.
  • Debris removal of burned furniture, fallen drywall and roofing, bagged or hauled to a dumpster.
  • Tear-out of materials that cannot be cleaned: charred drywall, wet insulation, burned flooring.
  • Ash and loose soot removal with HEPA vacuums, so it is not smeared into surfaces.
  • Air cleaning with HEPA air scrubbers while work goes on.
  • Photo documentation of every room for your insurance claim.

The fire damage cleanup process

  1. Release and safety. Wait for fire officials to release the building. The U.S. Fire Administration says not to turn utilities back on yourself.
  2. Secure the structure. Plywood over openings, tarps over roof holes, and temporary bracing if a wall or ceiling is weak.
  3. Remove water. Firefighting water is pumped and extracted, then the structure is dried and checked with moisture meters.
  4. Test before tear-out. In older homes, suspect materials are tested for asbestos, and paint is treated as lead-containing until tested.
  5. Remove debris and burned materials. Crews cut out charred and soaked drywall, insulation and flooring under containment.
  6. HEPA vacuum and air scrub. Loose ash and soot come off first so the next stage, soot removal, does not smear it.
  7. Hand off to cleaning and rebuild. The site is ready for smoke damage restoration and then repairs.

Why firefighting water is part of the cleanup

Fire hoses put a large amount of water into a house very quickly. That water soaks into subfloors, runs into the floor below and wicks up drywall. It also mixes with soot and ash into a dirty slurry. Leave it, and you add mold and swollen floors to the fire damage. That is why drying starts on day one, with extraction, air movers, dehumidifiers and daily moisture readings.

Lead, asbestos and ash: hazards in fire debris

Fire debris is not ordinary trash. The EPA says ash from burned structures is generally more hazardous than forest ash, and warns against dry sweeping or using a leaf blower. In homes built before 1978, painted surfaces may contain lead; the EPA requires paid contractors who disturb that paint to follow lead-safe work practices. The EPA also says suspect materials such as old floor or ceiling tile should be sampled by a trained, accredited asbestos inspector before renovation disturbs them.

Kitchen fire cleanup

Kitchen fires are the most common home fires. Cooking is the leading cause of home fires, according to the NFPA. Even a small grease fire leaves sticky, oily residue on cabinets, the range hood, ceiling and walls, and protein residue from burned food that is nearly invisible but smells strongly. Cabinet interiors, the hood duct and nearby rooms all need cleaning, not only the stove area.

What affects the cost of fire damage cleanup

  • How much water was used and how long it sat before extraction.
  • The amount of debris and the number of dumpsters needed.
  • How much drywall, insulation and flooring must be torn out.
  • Whether asbestos or lead testing and abatement are needed.
  • Access: stairs, crawl spaces, attics and distance to the dumpster.

Get a written estimate and send it to your adjuster before tear-out starts, unless an emergency step cannot wait.

DIY or call a professional?

You can handle a pan fire that left light smoke on one wall: open windows, wear gloves and a mask, and wipe hard surfaces with a mild detergent. Call a professional when any of these is true:

  • The fire department responded, or anything structural burned.
  • Water from fire hoses soaked floors, walls or the room below.
  • Soot is on surfaces in rooms far from the fire.
  • The home was built before 1978 or has old tile, plaster or pipe insulation.
  • You plan to file an insurance claim and need documentation.

Insurance and paperwork

Keep the fire department's incident report number, photograph every room before anything is moved, and save every receipt. The U.S. Fire Administration lists these as things your insurer may ask for. Ask your insurer about additional living expenses if you cannot stay in the home during cleanup.
