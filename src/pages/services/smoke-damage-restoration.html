Quick answer: Smoke damage restoration removes the residue, staining and odor smoke leaves on walls, ceilings, HVAC ducts and belongings after a fire. Crews test each surface, clean it with the right dry or wet method, run HEPA air scrubbers and seal what cleaning cannot fully fix. Acidic residue keeps discoloring surfaces, so it should start within days.

What smoke damage restoration includes

  • Pre-testing of each surface to see how the residue responds to dry and wet cleaning.
  • HEPA air scrubbing to capture fine particles while crews work.
  • Structure cleaning of walls, ceilings, trim, cabinets, windows and fixtures.
  • HVAC and duct cleaning, plus new filters, because the system spreads smoke through the house.
  • Contents cleaning on site or after a pack-out.
  • Odor control with thermal fogging, hydroxyl generators or ozone where needed.
  • Sealing of cleaned surfaces with a stain- and odor-blocking primer before paint.

Types of smoke damage

The fire's fuel and temperature decide the kind of residue, and the residue decides the cleaning method.

The smoke damage restoration process

  1. Inspect and map. The crew checks every room, the attic and the HVAC system to find where smoke traveled, not only where it is visible.
  2. Test surfaces. Small test spots show whether a surface needs dry cleaning first or can be wet-cleaned.
  3. Contain and filter the air. Plastic sheeting isolates work areas and HEPA air scrubbers run throughout.
  4. Dry clean first. HEPA vacuums and dry chemical sponges lift loose residue without smearing it.
  5. Wet clean. Detergents, degreasers or solvents matched to the residue clean walls, ceilings, cabinets and trim.
  6. Clean the HVAC system. Ducts, registers and the air handler are cleaned, and filters replaced.
  7. Deodorize and seal. Remaining odor is treated, then surfaces are primed with a sealer and repainted.

The ANSI/IICRC S700 standard, first published in 2025, sets out these steps for professional fire and smoke damage restoration.

Why smoke damage gets worse over time

Smoke residue is acidic and oily. In the first hours it can yellow plastics and stain grout. Over the next days it can etch glass, tarnish metal fixtures and discolor paint and upholstery. Electronics can corrode inside. Odor also sinks deeper into carpet, insulation and wood the longer it sits. That is why cleaning soon after the fire usually saves more than waiting for the insurance check.

Smoke damage in rooms far from the fire

Smoke follows air flow. It rises, moves toward cooler surfaces and gets pulled through return vents. That is why you may see dark lines on ceilings along joists, gray shadows around outlets and doors, and soot on the filter of an HVAC system in a room that did not burn. A good inspection checks closets, cabinet interiors, the attic and wall cavities near the fire.

Wildfire smoke damage

Wildfire smoke can damage a house that never caught fire. Ash and fine particles come in through vents, gaps around doors and windows, and the HVAC system. The EPA advises against dry sweeping or using a leaf blower on ash. Use a HEPA-type vacuum, mist surfaces lightly or wipe with a damp cloth instead. The EPA also notes that dry methods, such as vacuuming or "soot" or "chem" sponges, can work for thick deposits. Professional cleaning adds duct cleaning and odor treatment for homes with heavy deposits.

What affects the cost of smoke damage restoration

  • How many rooms and square feet the smoke reached.
  • The type of residue: wet, protein and fuel-oil residue take longer than dry smoke.
  • Ceiling height and textured or porous surfaces.
  • Duct length and the HVAC system's condition.
  • How many belongings need cleaning.

DIY or call a professional?

Light smoke film from a single candle or a small pan fire can often be cleaned yourself. The American Red Cross suggests a mild soap or detergent for walls, furniture and floors, wearing rubber gloves. Call a professional when the smoke spread past one room, when residue is oily or smeary, when the smell returns after cleaning, or when the HVAC system ran during the fire.

Insurance notes

Photograph soot on walls and ceilings before cleaning, keep receipts for anything you buy, and tell your adjuster about rooms that look clean but smell of smoke. Smoke damage is easy to miss in a quick inspection, and a written scope from a restoration crew helps document it.
