Commercial Fire Damage Restoration

Quick answer: Commercial fire damage restoration secures, cleans and rebuilds offices, stores, restaurants, rental buildings and warehouses after a fire. It covers board-up, water removal, soot and smoke cleaning, HVAC and odor work, contents and inventory, and repairs, planned around reopening. Smoke often damages far more floor space than the fire did, so fast cleaning limits losses.

What commercial fire restoration includes

  • Emergency response: board-up, roof tarping, fencing and site security.
  • Water removal from sprinklers and fire hoses, and structural drying.
  • Soot and smoke cleaning of walls, ceilings, open-web joists, ducts and fixtures.
  • HVAC cleaning of ductwork and air handlers that spread smoke through the building.
  • Odor removal with thermal fogging, hydroxyl or ozone in vacant areas.
  • Contents, inventory and equipment cleaning, or documentation of what is lost.
  • Repairs and build-back of structure, systems and finishes.
  • Documentation for the owner's, tenant's and business interruption claims.

The commercial fire restoration process

  1. Release and secure. After the fire marshal releases the site, crews board up, tarp, fence and set up security.
  2. Stop secondary damage. Sprinkler and hose water is removed and drying begins; sensitive equipment is protected or moved.
  3. Assess and phase. The building is mapped by damage level so undamaged areas can be cleaned and reopened first.
  4. Contain. Work zones are sealed off with negative air and HEPA filtration to protect occupied areas.
  5. Clean. Structure, systems and contents are cleaned to the methods in the ANSI/IICRC S700 standard.
  6. Deodorize. Odor treatment runs after hours or in vacant zones. The EPA warns that ozone can damage the lungs, so ozone is used only in empty spaces.
  7. Rebuild and turn over. Repairs, inspections and a final walk-through with the owner and tenants.

Restaurant and commercial kitchen fires

Cooking is the leading cause of fires in homes, according to the NFPA, and grease fires are just as common in commercial kitchens. They leave heavy grease and protein residue on hoods, ducts, walls and ceilings, and in walk-in coolers. Food and packaging exposed to smoke must be discarded. Restaurants also need the hood system cleaned and, in most places, inspected before reopening.

Business interruption and the claim

Commercial losses often have three parts: the building, the contents and the lost income. Business interruption coverage, if your policy includes it, may pay lost profits and continuing expenses while you are closed. Fast restoration shortens that period, and detailed documentation, such as moisture logs, photos and inventories, supports every part of the claim.

Multifamily and rental buildings

A fire in one apartment can push smoke into halls, stairwells and neighboring units through shared walls and ventilation. Owners and managers need a plan for tenant access, temporary relocation and communication. Each tenant's renters policy covers their belongings and extra living costs. The building's policy covers the structure and common areas.

What affects the cost of commercial fire restoration

  • Square footage affected by smoke, not only by fire.
  • Ceiling height, open structure and ductwork length.
  • Residue type, from grease to burned plastics.
  • Equipment, inventory and records to clean or document.
  • After-hours work and phasing to stay open.
  • Asbestos or lead testing and abatement.

Planning ahead for business owners

Keep a current list of equipment and inventory, a copy of your insurance policies off site, and emergency contacts for staff and tenants. Know where your utility shut-offs are. After a fire, the U.S. Fire Administration's advice for homes applies to businesses too: contact your insurer right away, keep every receipt and secure the property so no one can enter.

Commercial fire damage questions

How fast can a business reopen after a fire?

It depends on how much of the building burned and whether the fire marshal, building department and, for restaurants, health inspectors must sign off. A small kitchen fire may mean days; a fire that damaged structure and systems can mean months. Phased work can reopen undamaged areas first.

Can parts of a building stay open during fire restoration?

Often, if fire officials and the building department allow it. Crews isolate work areas with containment and negative air, run HEPA air scrubbers and schedule noisy or dusty work after hours where possible.

Who pays for commercial fire damage restoration?

Usually the property insurance of whoever owns the damaged part: the building owner's policy for the structure and the tenant's policy for their contents and improvements. Business interruption coverage, if you have it, may pay lost income while you are closed.

What about tenants in a rental or apartment building after a fire?

The owner handles the building, and each tenant's renters policy covers their own belongings and extra living costs. Landlords should secure the building, document the damage and keep tenants informed about access and timelines.

Do commercial jobs need special testing?

Often. Older commercial buildings may contain asbestos in flooring, ceiling tile or pipe insulation, and federal and state rules apply to renovation and demolition in many commercial buildings. Testing comes before tear-out.
