How to Deal With an Insurance Adjuster After a House Fire

By SootMend Editorial · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: To deal with an insurance adjuster after a house fire, get a full copy of your policy, document every room before cleaning, keep a written log of every conversation, and walk the house with the adjuster so nothing is missed. Don't guess, don't rush to settle, and get your own detailed repair estimate to compare. Put disputes and requests in writing.

The adjuster's visit can feel like a test you didn't study for. You're exhausted, the house smells like smoke, and someone is measuring rooms and deciding what your loss is worth. Adjusters handle claims every day; most homeowners face one only once. A little preparation evens that out. A clear, written repair scope from a fire damage repair contractor is one of the best tools you can bring to the table.

This is general information, not legal or insurance advice. For a large or disputed claim, consider talking with an attorney or a public adjuster.

Who is the insurance adjuster?

The adjuster inspects the damage, reviews your policy and recommends what the insurer pays. There are three kinds:

TypeWorks forPaid by
Staff adjusterYour insurance companyThe insurer (salary)
Independent adjusterYour insurer, under contractThe insurer (per claim)
Public adjusterYouYou, usually a percentage of the claim

Most adjusters are professionals trying to close a fair claim. But staff and independent adjusters work for the insurer, and United Policyholders notes that partial losses can be low priority for overloaded adjusters, especially after big disasters.

How to deal with an insurance adjuster: prepare for the visit

  1. Get your full policy. United Policyholders says to read the whole policy, not just the declarations page, and note deadlines, duties and limits.
  2. Document before cleaning. Photograph and video every room, ceiling, closet and cabinet. Include smoke film on walls, soot on vents and odor notes.
  3. Protect the property. Board-up, tarping and water removal show you took reasonable steps to prevent more damage. Keep the invoices.
  4. Start a claim diary. Record each conversation: date, name, title and what was said.
  5. Get the fire report. Ask the fire department for the report number.
  6. Start your contents list. Even a rough room-by-room list helps.
  7. Get a contractor's scope. A restoration contractor can inspect and write a line-item estimate to compare with the adjuster's.

During the inspection: what to do and say

  • Walk every room together, including the attic, basement, garage and crawl space.
  • Point out smoke damage far from the fire: closets, upstairs rooms, inside cabinets and the HVAC system.
  • Mention health concerns. United Policyholders advises telling the adjuster right away if anyone has asthma or allergies, since soot can aggravate them.
  • Stick to facts. Don't guess at the cause or the age of items. "I don't know yet" is a fine answer.
  • Ask questions. Ask what happens next, when you'll get the estimate and how ALE works.
  • Take notes and ask for anything important in writing.

What to do if the adjuster's estimate is too low

  1. Compare line by line. Put the adjuster's estimate next to your contractor's. Look for missing rooms, cleaning steps, odor work, code upgrades and HVAC cleaning.
  2. Send a written response. List each difference with photos and the contractor's scope. Ask the adjuster to explain each line they didn't include.
  3. Ask for a reinspection with your contractor present.
  4. Get independent help. United Policyholders suggests contractors, structural engineers or an industrial hygienist for air quality, and paying for independent inspections when there's a dispute.
  5. Escalate. Ask for a supervisor, use the policy's appraisal clause, or file a complaint with your state insurance department. Each state page lists its regulator.

Contents, inventory and depreciation

The contents part of a fire claim is often the hardest. United Policyholders recommends building an inventory with item age and cost, updating it as you remember things, and challenging depreciation. Some states, including California and Colorado, require insurers to offer part of the contents limit without a full itemized list. Replacement cost policies usually pay the rest after you replace items and send receipts.

Living expenses while you wait

If you can't live at home, ask the adjuster how your loss of use coverage works. The NAIC explains that additional living expenses pay costs above your normal living expenses, such as a hotel or rental, while the home is repaired. Keep every receipt and submit them on a regular schedule.

Should you hire a public adjuster?

A public adjuster represents you, prepares the claim and negotiates with the insurer, usually for a percentage of what you collect. Most states license public adjusters, and some cap their fees. A public adjuster can be worth it for large losses, complex contents claims or a stalled negotiation. For a small, clear claim, the fee may cost more than it gains. Check the license with your state insurance department before signing.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Throwing away damaged items before they're photographed and listed.
  • Signing a contract with a company that showed up uninvited before checking it.
  • Accepting a fast settlement before all damage is known.
  • Relying on phone calls with nothing in writing.
  • Missing the policy's deadlines for proof of loss or claims.

Insurance adjuster questions

What should I not say to an insurance adjuster after a fire?

Don't guess at the cause, admit fault, or agree that damage is minor before it has been inspected. Stick to facts you know, say "I don't know yet" when that's true, and don't accept a final number on the spot.

How long does an insurance adjuster have to inspect after a fire?

It depends on your state. Many states set deadlines for insurers to acknowledge a claim, inspect and respond. Your state insurance department can tell you the rules where you live.

Can I get my own estimate if I disagree with the adjuster?

Yes. Get a detailed, line-item estimate from a restoration contractor and send it to the adjuster with photos. United Policyholders suggests independent inspections and reports when there's a dispute over damage or repairs.

Should I hire a public adjuster after a house fire?

It can help on large or complex losses, or when talks have stalled. A public adjuster works for you, usually for a percentage of the claim, and most states license them. For small, straightforward claims, the fee may outweigh the gain.

What is appraisal in a home insurance claim?

Many policies have an appraisal clause for disputes over the amount of loss. Each side picks an appraiser, and an umpire settles differences. It's usually faster and cheaper than a lawsuit; read your policy for the exact process.
