Fire Damage Restoration Cost: What Drives the Price

By SootMend Editorial · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Fire damage restoration cost depends on how much of the home smoke and fire reached, the type of soot, how much water firefighters used, what must be torn out and rebuilt, how many belongings need cleaning, and local labor and code rules. There is no fair flat price. A line-item estimate after an inspection is the only reliable number, and insurance often pays most of it.

One of the first questions after a fire is "How much will this cost?" It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that it depends. A small stovetop fire and a fire that burns through a roof are different jobs, even in the same house. Instead of guessing at prices, this guide explains what drives the cost up or down, how to read an estimate, and how insurance pays. For a written scope on your own home, start with an inspection for fire damage repair.

This is general information, not insurance advice. Your policy decides what's covered.

Fire damage restoration cost by stage

Costs build up in stages. Knowing them helps you read the estimate.

  1. Emergency services: board-up, roof tarping and site security. Cost depends on the number of openings and roof access.
  2. Water removal and drying: equipment days, labor and monitoring.
  3. Tear-out and debris: labor, disposal fees and dumpsters.
  4. Soot and smoke cleaning: priced by area and surface, with more for oily soot, high ceilings and textured surfaces.
  5. Contents: pack-out labor, inventory, cleaning methods, storage time and pack-back.
  6. Odor removal: duct cleaning, fogging or ozone, sealing primers.
  7. Repairs: framing, roofing, drywall, electrical, flooring, cabinets and paint, plus permits.

Why there's no honest flat price

Price lists online often show wide ranges because the jobs behind them are so different. A range can't tell you whether your soot is dry or oily, whether insulation holds smoke, or whether your city requires code upgrades. Any number given before someone sees the damage is a guess. The cost that matters is the line-item scope for your home.

How to read a fire damage estimate

Most restoration estimates list each task by room, with a quantity, unit and price. Check these points:

  • Every room is listed, including closets, attic and garage.
  • HVAC cleaning is included if the system ran during or after the fire.
  • Soot type matches the cleaning method (oily soot priced as oily).
  • Odor treatment and sealing are listed where smell remains.
  • Contents are scoped separately from the building.
  • Code upgrades and permits are called out.
  • Testing for lead or asbestos is included in older homes.

United Policyholders notes that adjuster inspections alone are often not enough for serious losses, and that policyholders are generally entitled to a uniform and consistent appearance after repairs. That can matter when only part of a floor or cabinet run was damaged.

How insurance pays for fire damage restoration

For a covered fire, insurance usually pays restoration costs up to your limits, minus your deductible. The NAIC explains that a homeowners policy can insure on replacement cost (the cost to rebuild) or actual cash value (market value), and that you pay the deductible before the insurer pays.

Coverage partWhat it pays toward
DwellingCleaning and repairing the house and attached structures
Other structuresDetached garages, sheds and fences
Personal propertyCleaning or replacing belongings
Loss of use (ALE)Extra living costs while you can't live at home

Replacement cost policies often pay in two parts: the actual cash value first, then the rest once repairs or replacements are done. Ordinance or law coverage, if you have it, helps with code upgrade costs. See does homeowners insurance cover fire damage for details.

Costs that are easy to miss

  • Temporary housing, meals and laundry while you're out of the house.
  • Storage fees for packed-out belongings.
  • Code upgrades that weren't needed when the home was built.
  • Lead or asbestos testing and safe removal in older homes. The EPA requires lead-safe work practices for paid work on painted surfaces in homes built before 1978.
  • Landscaping, fences and outdoor items damaged by heat or crews.

How to keep fire damage costs down

  • Start board-up, water removal and soot cleaning quickly.
  • Keep the HVAC off until it's checked, so soot doesn't spread.
  • Don't wipe soot with water; smeared soot costs more to fix.
  • Save belongings that clean well instead of replacing them.
  • Get the scope right the first time with full photos and notes.

Local note: Labor, permits and code rules vary widely. Costs run differently in high-cost metros in California and New York, in older housing in Massachusetts, and on remote jobs in Alaska.

Restoration cost questions

Why don't you list prices for fire damage restoration?

Because every fire is different and a price without seeing the damage would be a guess. Cost depends on size, soot type, water, materials, local labor rates and codes. A written, line-item estimate after an inspection is the only reliable number.

Does insurance pay for fire damage restoration?

Usually, for sudden fires covered by your policy, up to your limits and after your deductible. The insurer pays based on its own estimate or an agreed scope, and replacement cost policies often pay in two parts.

Do I have to use the restoration company my insurer recommends?

In most cases, no. You can usually choose your own contractor, though the insurer must agree on the scope and price. Check your policy and your state's rules.

Is smoke damage cheaper to fix than fire damage?

Often, because cleaning costs less than rebuilding. But smoke can reach far more rooms than the fire did, so a whole-house smoke job can cost more than repairing one burned room.

Can I lower the cost of fire damage restoration?

Start mitigation quickly so water and soot don't cause more damage, keep belongings that clean well, and document everything so the scope is complete the first time. Delays and missed damage usually cost more.
